1. Data Communication

Data communication is about moving data reliably and efficiently between two points, whatever the physical medium.

Key ideas

  • Components of a data communication system: sender, receiver, a message, a transmission medium, and a protocol governing the exchange.
  • Simplex, half-duplex, duplex: simplex sends in one direction only; half-duplex sends both ways but not simultaneously; full duplex sends both ways at once.
  • Analog vs. digital signals: analog signals vary continuously; digital signals take discrete levels — more robust to noise but need conversion for analog media.
  • Noiseless vs. noisy channels: channel capacity is bounded by bandwidth alone for a noiseless channel (Nyquist), and further limited by the signal-to-noise ratio for a noisy channel (Shannon).
  • Bandwidth, throughput, latency: bandwidth is the channel's theoretical capacity; throughput is the actual achieved data rate; latency is the delay before data arrives.
  • Encoding & modulation: digital-to-digital, digital-to-analog, analog-to-digital, and analog-to-analog conversions prepare data for a given medium.
  • Baseband vs. broadband transmission: baseband sends a digital signal directly, unmodulated, over the whole medium (one channel at a time); broadband modulates signals onto different frequency bands, letting multiple channels share one medium.
  • Multiplexing: combines multiple signals for transmission over a single shared medium (e.g. FDM, TDM), improving medium utilization.
  • Transmission media: guided (twisted pair, coaxial, fiber optic) vs. unguided (radio, microwave, infrared) media.
  • Transmission errors & handling: noise/interference can corrupt bits in transit; error-handling mechanisms (detection codes, retransmission, correction codes) recover from or catch these errors.

2. Computer Networks

Networks are classified by their physical layout (topology) and by the geographic scale they cover.

Key ideas

  • Network topologies: star, bus, ring, mesh, and hybrid layouts trade off cost, fault tolerance, and ease of extension.
  • LAN (Local Area Network): covers a small area (building/campus) with high speed and low latency.
  • MAN (Metropolitan Area Network): spans a city-sized area, larger than a LAN but smaller than a WAN.
  • WAN (Wide Area Network): spans large geographic distances, often connecting multiple LANs across cities or countries.
  • Wireless networks & the Internet: wireless networks remove the need for physical cabling between nodes; the Internet is the global "network of networks" interconnecting countless smaller networks via common protocols.

3. Network Models

Layered models organize the many jobs a network must do into independent, stackable pieces.

Key ideas

  • Layered architecture: each layer provides services to the layer above and uses services from the layer below, letting layers evolve independently.
  • OSI Reference Model: 7 layers — Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, Application — each with its own protocols and responsibilities.
  • TCP/IP protocol suite: the model actually used on the Internet, with fewer layers (commonly 4-5: Link, Internet, Transport, Application), roughly mapping onto the OSI layers.
  • Addressing: physical (MAC) addresses identify a network interface; logical (IP) addresses identify a host on a network; port addresses identify a process on a host; specific (application) addresses (e.g. email address, URL) identify a service at a human-readable level.
  • Switching techniques: circuit switching (dedicated path for a call's duration), packet switching (data broken into packets routed independently), and message switching (whole message stored-and-forwarded) each trade off efficiency and latency differently.

4. Functions of OSI and TCP/IP Layers

This is where layer responsibilities become concrete protocols and mechanisms — from framing bits into frames up through routing and reliable delivery.

Key ideas

  • Framing, error detection/correction: the data link layer wraps bits into frames with markers/lengths, and adds checksums/CRCs to detect (or, with extra redundancy, correct) transmission errors.
  • Flow & error control: flow control (e.g. sliding window protocols) paces a fast sender to a slower receiver; error control retransmits lost/corrupted frames; HDLC is a classic bit-oriented data-link protocol implementing these.
  • Multiple access methods: CSMA/CD (sense, transmit, detect collisions, back off — classic Ethernet) and CSMA/CA (avoid collisions before they happen — used in wireless, where collisions are hard to detect); reservation, polling, token passing coordinate access without contention; FDMA, CDMA, TDMA divide a shared channel by frequency, code, or time respectively.
  • Network devices: hubs/repeaters (physical layer), switches/bridges (data link), routers (network layer); backbone networks interconnect multiple LANs; VLANs logically segment a physical LAN into separate broadcast domains.
  • IPv4 addressing: 32-bit addresses split into network and host portions; classful addressing used fixed class boundaries (A/B/C/D/E), while classless (CIDR) addressing allows flexible-length prefixes.
  • Datagrams, fragmentation, checksum: IP packets (datagrams) may be fragmented to fit a link's maximum size, and carry a checksum to catch header corruption.
  • IPv6: uses 128-bit addresses (vastly larger space than IPv4's 32 bits) with a simplified, fixed-size header.
  • ARP maps a logical (IP) address to its corresponding physical (MAC) address on a local network.
  • Direct vs. indirect delivery & routing: direct delivery sends to a host on the same network; indirect delivery routes through intermediate routers; routing algorithms (distance-vector, link-state) compute the paths packets take.
  • TCP, UDP, SCTP: TCP is connection-oriented, reliable, and ordered, with flow/error/congestion control; UDP is connectionless and unreliable but low-overhead; SCTP combines features of both, supporting multiple streams/multi-homing.

5. World Wide Web (WWW)

The Web layers human-friendly naming and hypertext on top of the Internet's raw packet delivery.

Key ideas

  • URL (Uniform Resource Locator): a structured address (protocol, host, path) identifying a specific resource on the Web.
  • DNS (Domain Name Service): a distributed, hierarchical system that resolves human-readable domain names to IP addresses (and vice versa).
  • Electronic mail architecture: mail user agents compose/read mail; mail transfer agents relay it between servers using standard protocols.
  • SMTP, POP, IMAP: SMTP sends/relays mail between servers; POP3 typically downloads mail to a single client (often removing it from the server); IMAP keeps mail synchronized on the server, accessible from multiple devices.
  • TELNET & FTP: TELNET provides remote terminal access to another machine; FTP transfers files between a client and a server.

6. Network Security

Network security protects data and systems from unauthorized access, disclosure, and disruption as it travels across shared, often untrusted networks.

Key ideas

  • Malware: viruses, worms, and other malicious software that spread and cause harm across networked systems.
  • Cryptography vs. steganography: cryptography makes a message's content unreadable to outsiders; steganography hides the very existence of a message (e.g. embedded inside an image).
  • Secret-key (symmetric) algorithms: sender and receiver share one secret key for both encryption and decryption (e.g. AES) — fast, but key distribution is a challenge.
  • Public-key (asymmetric) algorithms: use a mathematically linked key pair — a public key (shared openly) and a private key (kept secret) — solving the key-distribution problem (e.g. RSA), at higher computational cost.
  • Digital signatures: use a sender's private key to sign data, letting anyone verify authenticity, integrity, and non-repudiation with the corresponding public key.
  • VPNs create an encrypted tunnel across a public network, letting remote users/sites communicate as if on a private network.
  • Firewalls monitor and control incoming/outgoing traffic based on defined security rules, forming a barrier between trusted and untrusted networks.

7. Mobile Technology

Mobile and wireless technologies extend networking beyond fixed cabling, introducing mobility, air-interface sharing, and location management.

Key ideas

  • GSM & CDMA: GSM (widely used 2G standard) primarily uses TDMA/FDMA to share the channel; CDMA gives every user a unique code, letting many users share the same frequency simultaneously.
  • Mobile computing architecture & middleware/gateways: mobile computing systems bridge wired backend infrastructure and wireless mobile devices, often via middleware that handles disconnection, low bandwidth, and device diversity.
  • Mobile IP: lets a device keep the same IP address (and stay reachable) as it moves between different networks, using home/foreign agents to forward traffic.
  • Communication satellites & wireless topologies: satellites relay signals over huge areas; wireless topologies range from fixed cellular grids to ad hoc, infrastructure-less arrangements.
  • Cellular topology: coverage is divided into hexagonal cells, each served by a base station, with frequencies reused across non-adjacent cells.
  • Mobile Ad hoc Networks (MANETs): devices communicate directly, peer-to-peer, without relying on fixed infrastructure — routes may need to be discovered dynamically as nodes move.
  • Wireless LANs, geolocation, GPRS & SMS: Wi-Fi (wireless LAN) provides local wireless connectivity; geolocation systems (e.g. GPS-assisted) determine device position; GPRS adds packet-data service to GSM networks; SMS delivers short text messages over the signaling channel.

8. Cloud Computing and IoT

Cloud computing delivers computing resources as a service over the network, while IoT extends networking to everyday physical devices.

Key ideas

  • SaaS, PaaS, IaaS: SaaS delivers complete applications over the internet; PaaS provides a platform for developers to build/deploy apps without managing infrastructure; IaaS provides raw computing infrastructure (VMs, storage, networking) that the customer configures further.
  • Public vs. private cloud: a public cloud shares infrastructure across many customers over the internet; a private cloud is dedicated to a single organization for greater control/security.
  • Virtualization & virtual servers: abstracts physical hardware into flexible, on-demand virtual machines — the technical foundation cloud services are built on.
  • Cloud & database storage: data is stored redundantly across a provider's infrastructure, accessed as a service rather than tied to specific physical hardware.
  • Resource management & SLAs: providers dynamically allocate/scale resources to meet demand, governed by a Service Level Agreement defining guaranteed availability/performance.
  • Basics of IoT: everyday physical objects (sensors, appliances, vehicles) are equipped with network connectivity, letting them sense, exchange, and act on data.
